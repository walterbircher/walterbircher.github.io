Our Research Strategy

cureEDMD pursues two complementary approaches to developing treatments and cures for EDMD. These are research and development goals; benefit for people with EDMD must be established through appropriate studies.

Repurposed Drugs

Investigate whether existing medicines can address EDMD disease pathways and advance promising candidates toward clinical evaluation.

Finding a mechanism, or “target”, in skeletal or cardiac muscle cells that could be influenced by a therapeutic drug and then searching for a drug that has already been FDA-approved for another indication that addresses the target. While there is no guarantee such a drug, or combination of drugs, can be found, this is a much quicker and less costly approach than trying to develop a new drug.

Significant progress has already been made on this approach, facilitated by cureEDMD-associated funding, to the point that drugs may be screened for efficacy within the next few years. More broadly, processes for screening for existing drugs that help against disease targets are advancing, including through use of artificial intelligence (AI) techniques. 

Gene Therapy

Support approaches addressing the underlying genetic causes of EDMD.

Gene therapy for EDMD. By replacing or repairing the gene that is missing or defective it might be possible to treat or, if done early enough, even cure the disease. Gene therapy research is very costly, but gene therapy for EDMD can benefit from all of the work that has already been done for other muscle diseases, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Fortunately the defective EMD gene in X-linked EDMD, which encodes for emerin, is very small at only about 1200 base pairs, and can easily fit into existing delivery vectors already developed for other diseases. For autosomal dominant EDMD caused by LMNA gene mutations, repairing the gene will more likely be required.

Developing new drugs and broader treatment potential

Another approach would be to try to develop a new drug that addresses targets identified in EDMD. This could include targets downstream of the implicated genes themselves. This might have a higher probability of success than searching for existing drugs that are effective, but it is very costly (probably $10 million or more) and most likely very time consuming, on the order of several years at least even with sufficient funding. cureEDMD intends to seek or help motivate such funding once reasonably high-probability disease targets are identified.

It is possible that a drug that is effective in EDMD would also help in related diseases such as limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, due to similar disease mechanisms. This might increase the interest of an existing pharmaceutical or biotech company, or a self- or capital-markets-funded startup, to pursue a therapeutic drug for EDMD.

Genetics and Natural History

Gisèle Bonne, PhD

Focus: EDMD genetics, laminopathies, disease models, and therapeutic development

Affiliation: Sorbonne Université, INSERM, and the Institut de Myologie, Paris

Gisèle Bonne is one of the central figures in modern EDMD research. In a landmark 1999 Nature Genetics paper, she led the discovery that pathogenic variants in LMNA cause autosomal-dominant EDMD, establishing the connection between lamin A/C and inherited muscle disease. That finding transformed EDMD from a condition associated primarily with emerin deficiency into part of a much broader family of nuclear-envelope disorders. Her group now combines genetics, international patient cohorts, disease modeling, biomarker research, and experimental therapy development for LMNA-related skeletal and cardiac disease.

Research profile

Francesco Muntoni, MD

Focus: Pediatric neuromuscular disease, natural history, clinical trials, and genetic therapies

Affiliation: University College London and Great Ormond Street Hospital, London

Francesco Muntoni is one of the world's most prominent pediatric neuromuscular clinicians and therapy-development leaders. His EDMD-relevant work includes the international LMNA natural-history study that defined patterns of progression in early-onset disease and helped establish trial-ready clinical measurements. More broadly, he has helped develop genetic therapies, outcome measures, and clinical-trial infrastructure for rare muscular dystrophies through the Dubowitz Neuromuscular Centre and UCL Genetic Therapy Accelerator. His importance to EDMD lies partly in his ability to translate rare-disease biology into practical clinical studies.

Research profile

Eric C. Schirmer, PhD

Focus: Cross-genotype EDMD pathways, nuclear-envelope biology, and biomarkers

Affiliation: University of Edinburgh

Eric Schirmer studies how nuclear-envelope proteins organize the genome and regulate tissue-specific gene expression. In a 2022-2023 study of cells from patients carrying variants in seven different EDMD-spectrum genes, his group identified recurring abnormalities in fibrosis, metabolism, muscle signaling, RNA splicing, and mitochondrial function. The study was important because it searched for shared disease pathways across genetically different forms of EDMD rather than assuming that every subtype has an entirely separate mechanism. These shared signatures could inform future biomarker panels and treatments that apply across multiple genotypes.

Cardiac Risk

Elizabeth M. McNally, MD, PhD

Focus: Muscular dystrophy genetics, inherited cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and precision medicine

Affiliation: Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, Chicago

Elizabeth McNally is a leading physician-scientist at the intersection of muscular dystrophy and inherited heart disease. Her laboratory uses patient genomics, engineered cells, and animal models to study genes including LMNA, SYNE1, and SYNE2 and to understand why people with related variants develop different degrees of muscle weakness, arrhythmia, and heart failure. Recent work from her group supports a multiple-hit model in which combinations of genetic variants can influence cardiomyopathy penetrance and severity. This broader modifier-gene perspective is especially relevant to the substantial clinical variability seen among people carrying the same EDMD-associated variant.

Research profile

Luisa Mestroni, MD

Focus: Familial cardiomyopathy, international registries, LMNA disease, and clinical translation

Affiliation: University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus

Luisa Mestroni is a major leader in the genetics of familial dilated cardiomyopathy and directs the International Familial Cardiomyopathy Registry. Her program connects disease-gene discovery with longitudinal patient follow-up, arrhythmia risk, heart failure, and experimental treatment. Her LMNA-related translational work helped bring inhibition of the p38 MAPK pathway into phase 2 clinical testing with ARRY-371797, moving a mechanism first identified in laboratory models into studies involving patients. Whether or not a particular therapy ultimately succeeds, this bench-to-clinic pathway is an important model for future EDMD drug development.

Research profile

Karim Wahbi, MD, PhD

Focus: Arrhythmia prediction, heart failure, cardiac surveillance, and patient registries

Affiliation: Cochin Hospital, AP-HP, and the Institut de Myologie, Paris

Karim Wahbi is among the most directly relevant cardiac researchers for people with EDMD. His landmark clinical contribution was leading the development and validation of the 2019 LMNA-risk VTA model, which estimates an individual's risk of life-threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmia using genetic and clinical variables. The model helped move LMNA cardiac care toward gene-specific risk assessment rather than relying exclusively on general cardiomyopathy criteria. His group also studies cardiac outcomes in EMD-related disease and leads registries for laminopathies and emerinopathies.

Featured Researchers

Dennis E. Discher, PhD

Focus: Lamin mechanobiology, tissue stiffness, and mechanically regulated cell behavior

Affiliation: University of Pennsylvania

Dennis Discher is one of the most influential researchers in cellular mechanobiology. In a landmark 2013 Science paper, his group showed that lamin A levels scale with the stiffness and mechanical demands of tissues and influence matrix-directed cell differentiation. The study provided a powerful general explanation for why abnormalities in lamin A/C can have their strongest effects in mechanically active tissues such as skeletal muscle and the heart. Although the work was not limited to EDMD, it fundamentally shaped how researchers think about lamins as adaptive regulators of nuclear mechanics.

Research profile

Tom Misteli, PhD

Focus: Nuclear architecture, genome organization, and lamin-chromatin interactions

Affiliation: National Cancer Institute, National Institutes of Health

Tom Misteli is a pioneer in genome cell biology and the study of how three-dimensional nuclear organization affects gene regulation and disease. His group has mapped changes in chromatin organization and lamin interactions caused by abnormal lamin A, particularly in progeria and other laminopathies. Although this work is not specific to EDMD, it established methods and concepts for understanding how LMNA variants can alter genome organization rather than acting only through mechanical weakness. This broader genome-organization perspective remains highly relevant to muscle differentiation and disease progression in EDMD.

Research profile

Antoine Muchir, PhD

Focus: Disease signaling and preclinical treatment of LMNA- and EMD-related cardiomyopathy

Affiliation: Sorbonne Université, INSERM, and the Institut de Myologie, Paris

Antoine Muchir was the lead experimental investigator on the influential study identifying early MAPK activation in an LMNA EDMD model. His subsequent work showed that pharmacologic inhibition of pathways such as ERK and JNK could reduce fibrosis, improve cardiac function, or delay cardiomyopathy in experimental models. These studies provided some of the earliest evidence that a molecular pathway downstream of lamin dysfunction could be therapeutically targeted. His group continues to investigate signaling, calcium regulation, fibrosis, and other modifiable mechanisms in striated-muscle laminopathies.

Research profile

Stephen G. Young, MD

Focus: Lamin biology, genetically engineered disease models, and nuclear structure

Affiliation: University of California, Los Angeles

Stephen Young's laboratory has developed and characterized influential genetic models of nuclear-lamin disease. In a widely cited 2006 study with Jan Lammerding and collaborators, the researchers demonstrated that lamins A and C, but not lamin B1, are major determinants of nuclear mechanical stiffness. The result clarified the specific structural role of A-type lamins and helped explain the nuclear fragility observed in LMNA muscular dystrophies. His mouse models and molecular studies remain important tools for research on laminopathies, cardiomyopathy, and nuclear-envelope function.

Research profile

Jan Lammerding, PhD

Focus: Nuclear mechanics, mechanotransduction, DNA damage, and muscle-cell injury

Investigating how nuclear-envelope proteins affect muscle cells and exploring LINC complex disruption as a potential treatment approach.

Affiliation: Cornell University

Jan Lammerding is a professor in the Meinig School of Biomedical Engineering and the Weill Institute for Cell and Molecular Biology at Cornell University. He was appointed as the Associate Director of the Meinig School of Biomedical Engineering in 2024. He obtained a Diplom Ingenieur degree in Mechanical Engineering in his native Germany and a Bachelor of Engineering degree from the Thayer School of Engineering at Dartmouth College. Subsequently, he completed his Ph.D. in Biological Engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology studying subcellular biomechanics and mechanotransduction signaling with Drs. Roger Kamm and Richard T. Lee (Brigham and Women’s Hospital/Harvard Medical School). Following a brief postdoctoral fellowship in Dr. Lee’s laboratory, he started his faculty career at Harvard Medical School/Brigham and Women’s Hospital while also teaching in the Department of Biological Engineering at the Massachusetts Institute for Technology before joining Cornell University in 2011.

Dr. Lammerding has published over 100 peer-reviewed articles, including in Nature, Science, and PNAS. He has won several prestigious awards, including the Keith Porter award of the American Society for Cell Biology (ASCB) for exceptional contributions to cell biology, a National Science Foundation CAREER Award, and the American Heart Association Scientist Development Grant. He was named one of the 2014 Young Innovators by the Cell and Molecular Bioengineering Journal. Dr. Lammerding is a Fellow of the Biomedical Engineering Society (BMES), the American Institute for Medical and Biological Engineering (AIMBE), and the American Society for Cell Biology (ASCB). The research in the Lammerding laboratory is supported by grants from the National Institutes of Health, the National Science Foundation, the Leducq Foundation, and the Volkswagen Foundation. 

The Lammerding laboratory at Cornell University is researching the cellular functions affected by emerin and lamins, the nuclear-lamina proteins that are missing or faulty in EDMD, with the goal to identify novel therapeutic strategies to intervene in the mechanisms responsible for EDMD to treat the disease. One promising area of the lab’s research is called “LINC complex disruption”. The Linker of Nucleoskeleton and Cytoskeleton (LINC) complex is a large multi-protein structure that physically connects the cell nucleus with the surrounding cellular structures. The lab has found that disrupting the LINC complex shows very promising improvements in muscle function both in laboratory-cultured diseased muscle cells and in mouse models of EDMD (i.e., mice genetically engineered to have similar mutations as in human EDMD patients, thereby serving as ‘preclinical’ models to study the disease and to test new treatment approaches). In collaboration with researchers at Stanford University, the University of Pennsylvania, Duke University and the Institute of Myology/INSERM (Paris, France), supported in part through Networks of Excellence funded by the Leducq Foundation, the Lammerding lab is working to identify drugs that can target the LINC complex or achieve similar benefits as LINC complex disruption.

In parallel, the Lammerding lab is exploring additional treatment approaches that could substitute or complement LINC complex disruption to result in even more effective improvements. As this work progresses, the lab is investigating whether existing, FDA-approved drugs could be used off-label to help EDMD patients.

The work of the Lammerding Lab has real potential to benefit EDMD patients in the coming years and is therefore a high priority for cureEDMD. Based on our current understanding of the disease mechanism, there is good reason to believe that any therapeutic progress on EDMD might also provide benefit for other laminopathies, including limb-girdle muscular dystrophy, congenital muscular dystrophy, and LMNA-associated dilated cardiomyopathy.

Research profile

Lori L. Wallrath, PhD

Focus: LMNA muscular dystrophy models, chromatin, redox biology, and therapeutic pathways

Affiliation: University of Iowa Carver College of Medicine

Lori Wallrath develops fruit-fly models carrying patient-associated lamin mutations to investigate skeletal-muscle and cardiac laminopathies. Her models have revealed abnormalities in nuclear organization, nucleo-cytoskeletal coupling, oxidative stress, protein aggregation, and muscle function, creating a tractable system for testing genetic and pharmacological modifiers. She also contributed to the influential 2020 study showing that mutant lamins cause mechanically induced nuclear-envelope rupture and DNA damage in skeletal muscle. Her work is particularly valuable because it connects fundamental lamin biology with rapid in vivo screening for potentially modifiable disease pathways.

Research profile

Howard J. Worman, MD

Focus: Nuclear-envelope biology, laminopathies, and LMNA-related cardiomyopathy

Studying disease pathways in heart and skeletal muscle and developing gene therapy approaches to restore emerin.

Affiliation: Columbia University

Howard J. Worman, M.D. is Professor of Medicine and Pathology and Cell Biology at Columbia University and Attending Physician at New York-Presbyterian Hospital Columbia University Irving Medical Center. Dr. Worman received his BA from Cornell University and MD from the University of Chicago. He did an internship and residency in internal medicine at New York Hospital-Cornell Medical Center and a postdoctoral research fellowship in cell biology at Rockefeller University, where he started his research on the nuclear envelope.

Dr. Worman is internationally recognized for his nearly 40 years of research on the nuclear envelope and its role in disease, including Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy (EDMD). In 1993, his laboratory characterized the human lamin A/C gene, which is mutated in autosomal dominant EDMD. In 1999, his group published one of the first studies of how emerin, the protein lacking in X-linked EDMD, is targeted to the nuclear envelope inner membrane. In 2013, his laboratory showed that emerin deficiency significantly worsened the muscular dystrophy in mice lacking lamina-associated polypeptide 1 from skeletal muscle, thus establishing the only vertebrate model system to date in which the effects of emerin disruption on muscle pathology can be studied (unlike humans, mice without emerin to not develop muscular dystrophy). Using various mouse and cellular models, Dr. Worman’s laboratory has conducted numerous studies dissecting gene expression and cell signaling abnormalities in heart and skeletal muscle that occur in EDMD. His laboratory has also tested several potential therapies in mouse models of EDMD.

Dr. Worman’s current research on EDMD is aimed at deciphering the common transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolomic alterations in heart cells with deletions of the genes encoding lamin A/C and emerin. This research could uncover potential therapeutic targets for  two different inherited types of EDMD. He is using mouse models to determine how depletion of lamin A/C from mature skeletal muscle fibers and satellite cells – the cells responsible for muscle regeneration – contribute to pathology in autosomal dominant EDMD. His laboratory is also working on developing gene therapy to restore emerin expression in heart and skeletal muscle that could be translated to patients with X-linked EDMD. 

Dr. Worman is an author on over 225 scientific publications. In 1998, he was elected to membership in the American Society for Clinical Investigator and, in 2010, to the Association of American Physicians. He has served as a member of numerous NIH review panels, the MDA Medical Advisory Committee and the NIH Action Plan for the Muscular Dystrophies Mechanisms of Muscular Dystrophy Working Group. In addition to his academic activities, Dr. Worman is a consultant for pharmaceutical, biotechnology and clinical genomics companies, including those developing gene therapies, other treatments and diagnostics for muscular dystrophies.


Research profile

Emerin

Giovanna Lattanzi, PhD

Focus: Emerin, laminopathies, muscle-cell mechanics, fibrosis, and gene correction

Affiliation: CNR Institute of Molecular Genetics "Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza," Bologna

Giovanna Lattanzi studies how emerin and lamin defects disrupt nuclear organization, cellular differentiation, cytoskeletal connections, and profibrotic signaling. In recent 2026 work, her group used CRISPR-corrected EDMD1 patient cells to show that restoring functional emerin rescued the positioning of proteins involved in centrosome movement, mechanosignaling, and muscle differentiation. The study provides direct evidence that correcting the underlying EMD variant can reverse measurable disease-associated cellular abnormalities. This makes her program particularly relevant to the transition from mechanism discovery toward mutation-correcting therapies.

Recent work Research profile

Katherine L. Wilson, PhD

Focus: Emerin, LEM-domain proteins, BAF, chromatin, and mechanotransduction

Affiliation: Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine

Katherine Wilson is one of the most influential researchers studying emerin, the protein absent or dysfunctional in X-linked EDMD1. Her laboratory established much of the molecular framework describing emerin's interactions with lamins, BAF, nuclear actin, nesprins, chromatin, and transcriptional regulators. More recent work showed that acute loss of emerin alters actomyosin connectivity and impairs cellular responses to changing mechanical environments, strengthening the connection between emerin and mechanotransduction. Together, these findings help explain how loss of a widely expressed nuclear protein can preferentially damage mechanically active muscle cells.

LINC Complex

Brian Burke, PhD

Focus: Nuclear-envelope architecture and the LINC complex

Affiliation: A*STAR Institute of Molecular and Cell Biology, Singapore

Brian Burke is one of the foundational figures in nuclear-envelope and LINC-complex biology. In a landmark 2006 paper with Didier Hodzic and colleagues, his group formalized the concept of the LINC complex, describing how SUN proteins and nesprins form a molecular bridge across the nuclear envelope. This framework transformed understanding of how the cytoskeleton applies force to the nucleus and provided the conceptual foundation for interpreting SYNE1-, SYNE2-, SUN1-, and SUN2-associated disease. His later work has examined nuclear positioning in muscle and the therapeutic potential of modifying LINC-complex connections.

Research profile

Gregg G. Gundersen, PhD

Focus: Cytoskeletal force transmission, nuclear movement, and LINC-complex mechanics

Affiliation: Columbia University

Gregg Gundersen is a prominent researcher in cytoskeletal organization and nuclear positioning. His laboratory helped provide direct experimental evidence that nesprin-SUN interactions transmit actin-generated forces across the nuclear envelope. He also contributed to the study showing that disease-associated SUN1 and SUN2 variants disrupt nucleo-cytoskeletal coupling and myonuclear organization. This work clarifies the mechanical consequences of variants affecting the inner and outer components of the LINC complex.

Research profile

Didier Hodzic, PhD

Focus: SUN proteins, nesprins, nuclear positioning, and muscle disease models

Affiliation: Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis

Didier Hodzic was a corresponding author on the foundational study defining the LINC complex and has made major contributions to understanding SUN-nesprin interactions. His group subsequently developed models in which disruption of nesprin-dependent nuclear positioning produced muscle abnormalities resembling EDMD. This work demonstrated that incorrect positioning of muscle-cell nuclei is not merely a visual feature of disease but can contribute directly to muscle dysfunction. His research provides an important bridge between the molecular architecture of the LINC complex and disease at the tissue level.

FHL1

Carsten G. Bönnemann, MD

Focus: Congenital and inherited muscle disease, FHL1 myopathies, and therapy development

Affiliation: St. Jude Children's Research Hospital, Memphis

Carsten Bönnemann is one of the world's leading authorities on congenital and inherited muscle disorders. In a landmark 2008 Journal of Clinical Investigation paper, he and his collaborators used proteomic and genetic approaches to identify FHL1 as the protein mutated in reducing-body myopathy. That discovery opened the modern field of FHL1-related muscle disease and provided the foundation for recognizing a broader spectrum that includes EDMD6. His program combines deep clinical phenotyping, pathology, genomics, disease models, and therapeutic development for rare muscle disorders.

Research profile

Ju Chen, PhD

Focus: FHL1 function, sarcomere signaling, biomechanical stress, and cardiac remodeling

Affiliation: University of California, San Diego

Ju Chen is a distinguished cardiovascular and muscle biologist who has helped define the normal function of FHL1. In an influential 2008 study, his group showed that FHL1 is part of a sarcomeric complex that senses biomechanical stress and promotes cardiac hypertrophy. Mice lacking Fhl1 had a reduced hypertrophic response and were protected from certain forms of pressure-induced cardiomyopathy. These results provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how FHL1 variants can cause skeletal myopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and abnormal responses to mechanical stress.

Research profile

Christian Windpassinger, MD

Focus: Human genetics and the expanding FHL1 disease spectrum

Affiliation: Medical University of Graz, Austria

Christian Windpassinger helped establish FHL1 as a human muscle-disease gene through the discovery of variants causing X-linked myopathy with postural muscle atrophy and generalized hypertrophy. This work demonstrated that different FHL1 variants could cause phenotypes extending beyond reducing-body myopathy and helped reveal the protein's unusually broad clinical spectrum. Subsequent studies connected FHL1 variants with EDMD, rigid-spine disease, cardiac involvement, and other overlapping syndromes. His research is particularly relevant to understanding why the location and molecular effect of an FHL1 variant can produce very different skeletal and cardiac manifestations.

Research profile

TMEM43

Yukiko K. Hayashi, MD, PhD

Focus: EDMD genetics, muscle pathology, and nuclear-envelope disease

Affiliation: Tokyo Medical University

Yukiko Hayashi was a senior investigator in the 2011 study linking TMEM43 variants to EDMD-related myopathy. Her research has helped connect genetic findings with muscle pathology, nuclear deformities, protein localization, and clinical presentation in EMD-, LMNA-, and TMEM43-associated disease. She has continued to investigate how abnormalities of nuclear-envelope proteins affect muscle-cell nuclei and why the resulting cellular abnormalities do not always correlate simply with clinical severity. Her work is especially valuable for interpreting the pathological consequences of rare EDMD-associated variants.

Research profile

Ichizo Nishino, MD, PhD

Focus: Muscle pathology, nuclear-envelope myopathies, and TMEM43-related EDMD

Affiliation: National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry, Tokyo

Ichizo Nishino is one of the world's leading researchers in muscle pathology and inherited myopathy. His group helped lead the landmark 2011 study identifying TMEM43 variants in patients with an EDMD-related myopathy, providing the principal basis for the EDMD7 classification. The study showed abnormal nuclear morphology and disturbed localization or interaction of nuclear-envelope proteins, directly connecting TMEM43 dysfunction with an EDMD-like skeletal-muscle phenotype. His broader work on emerinopathies and laminopathies integrates genetics, pathology, imaging, and clinical characterization.

Research profile

Terry-Lynn Young, PhD

Focus: TMEM43 genetics, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, and precision disease models

Affiliation: Memorial University of Newfoundland, Canada

Terry-Lynn Young led the genetics team responsible for a landmark 2008 discovery linking the TMEM43 p.S358L founder variant to a severe, highly penetrant form of arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy in Newfoundland families. The study documented exceptionally high risks of ventricular arrhythmia and premature death, particularly among affected men. Although this phenotype is generally classified as arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy rather than EDMD, it established TMEM43 as a major cardiac disease gene and remains central to understanding the cardiac risk associated with TMEM43 dysfunction. Her team has since moved toward patient-derived cardiomyocytes and gene-editing approaches to investigate disease mechanisms and potential treatments.
